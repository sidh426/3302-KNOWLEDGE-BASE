## Why these engines matter

For about fifteen years, BMW's everyday performance cars were built around one idea: a turbocharged 3.0-liter inline-six. Three engines in particular defined that stretch, and my car uses the newest of them. Understanding the difference helps explain why people talk about these engines the way they do, and why used BMW shoppers ask about the engine code before anything else.

## The three engines

| Engine | Appeared | Turbo layout | Typical output in a 3/4 Series |
|---|---|---|---|
| N54 | 2006 | Two turbochargers | about 300 hp (335i) |
| N55 | around 2009–2010 | One twin-scroll turbo | about 300 hp (335i, 435i) |
| B58 | 2015 | One twin-scroll turbo | about 320 hp (340i, 440i) |

### N54: the twin-turbo original

The N54 was BMW's first mass-produced turbocharged, direct-injection inline-six, and it used two small turbos. It was fast and easy to tune, which earned it a loyal following. It was also known for early problems, including failures of the high-pressure fuel pump and wastegate rattle, and that reputation still shapes how people shop for older cars.

### N55: simpler and more refined

The N55 replaced the two turbos with a single twin-scroll turbo and added Valvetronic variable valve lift. It was generally considered more reliable than the N54, although owners often had to deal with oil leaks from gaskets as the cars aged.

### B58: the one in my car

The B58 is the N55's successor and keeps the same layout, but it was designed with a stronger closed-deck block and fixes for many earlier weaknesses. The 440i is rated at about 320 horsepower and 330 lb-ft of torque, with the torque available from very low rpm, so the car pulls hard without needing to rev. It's smooth, and I've never had a major engine issue in about 90,000 miles.

> The B58 is such a good engine that Toyota used it in the GR Supra, which makes it a rare crossover between BMW and the [[jdm/index|JDM]] world I love.
