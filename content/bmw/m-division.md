## Where M came from

BMW M started in 1972 as BMW Motorsport GmbH, a small racing arm of the company. Its first true road car was the mid-engine M1 in 1978, but most enthusiasts date the modern M legend to the E30 M3 in 1986, which BMW built so it could go touring car racing. From that moment, the idea behind every M car was the same: take an ordinary BMW and re-engineer it for the track, then let people drive it on the street.

## The M3 and M4 through the years

The nameplate has changed a lot, and following it is a good way to understand how BMW performance evolved.

### Naturally aspirated era

- **E46 M3 (2001–2006):** a 3.2-liter inline-six (S54) making 333 horsepower. Many people still call it one of the best-sounding M cars.
- **E92 M3 (2007–2013):** the one I think of most, since it comes from the late-2000s era I grew up in. It used a 4.0-liter V8 (S65) making 414 horsepower and revved to around 8,400 rpm.

### Turbo era

- **F80 M3 and F82 M4 (2014 onward):** BMW switched to a twin-turbo 3.0-liter inline-six (S55) making 425 horsepower, and the coupe was renamed the M4 to match the new 4 Series. Purists complained about the quieter sound, but the torque was a big step forward.
- **G80 M3 and G82 M4 (2020 and 2021 onward):** newer S58 engine with more power. The large vertical kidney grille on the M4 was controversial when it was revealed.

## M versus M Sport versus M Performance

BMW uses the "M" badge in several ways, and the difference matters when you shop for one.

1. **M cars** (M3, M4, M2): the full performance cars with dedicated engines, brakes, and suspension.
2. **M Performance** models (like the M340i and M440i): the in-between option, using the regular engine family tuned for more power.
3. **M Sport packages**: mostly appearance and suspension upgrades on regular models. These don't change the engine.
